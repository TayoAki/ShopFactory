# Sun Protection for Darker Skin on the Golf Course

**Yes, Black women need sunscreen for golf.** The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/skin-cancer/types/common/melanoma/skin-color) says "dermatologists recommend sunscreen for everyone," and calls the idea that only people with light skin get skin cancer "a common misbelief."

Golf means hours in open sun. So the basics matter: **the right sunscreen**, enough of it, and a hat that covers your part.

## Do Black women need sunscreen for golf?

Yes. The AAD's answer to whether people with brown or Black skin need sunscreen is direct: "Yes. Dermatologists recommend sunscreen for everyone."

The AAD gives two reasons beyond skin cancer. If you have brown or Black skin, it says, the sun "can speed up how quickly your skin ages." And the sun's visible light can cause hyperpigmentation, which shows up as dark spots.

The golf sun guides we read don't mention darker skin. This one does, because Black women golfers are [who we make hats for](/pages/about-us).

The AAD's advice comes down to three layers: a tinted sunscreen, shade, and sun-protective clothing. On a golf course, your hat is part of that clothing. ## What sunscreen works on darker skin?

Look for a tinted, broad-spectrum, water-resistant sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher. The AAD recommends a "tinted sunscreen with iron oxide," because "iron oxide helps to protect your skin from the sun's visible light (a cause of dark spots)."

Along with iron oxide, the AAD says your tinted sunscreen should have:

- SPF 30 or higher
- Broad-spectrum protection
- Water resistance
- A non-comedogenic formula (one that won't clog pores) if you have oily skin
- "The ability to blend with your skin tone (avoids a white cast)"

For golf, add one more check. The Skin Cancer Foundation's [golf advice](https://www.skincancer.org/blog/sun-protection-habits-golf-course/) suggests a sport formula that is "sweat resistant and won't run into your eyes." Check the water resistance time on the label, too. The [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/tips-stay-safe-sun-sunscreen-sunglasses) says water resistance claims, for 40 or 80 minutes, tell you how long to expect the labeled SPF while swimming or sweating.

## How much sunscreen, and how often, on a round?

Use at least one ounce, put it on 30 minutes before you head out, and reapply every two hours, or more often if you're sweating heavily. That's the Skin Cancer Foundation's golf advice, and it applies to "all exposed areas." The [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/sun-protection/shade-clothing-sunscreen/how-to-apply-sunscreen) describes one ounce as "roughly the amount to fill a shot glass."

Reapplying is the step that's easy to forget once you're playing. The foundation's fix is simple: "designate a hole or two where you will always reapply." Here's one way to plan it:

- **Before you head out.** Apply 30 minutes ahead, on every patch of skin your clothes don't cover.
- **At the turn.** The break between the front nine and the back nine is an easy stop to remember.
- **Any time you've sweated through.** Don't wait for the two-hour mark.

Keep a stick or a small tube in your bag so it's there at the turn. The CDC notes that sunscreen exposed to high temperatures has a shorter shelf life, so don't leave it in a hot car between rounds. For your lips, the AAD recommends a lip balm with SPF 30 or higher.

## Where do golfers miss?

The spots people overlook are the ears, the back of the neck, the part and the hairline. The Skin Cancer Foundation's golf advice says to "cover your ears and neck with sunscreen as these areas are still exposed," even under a cap or visor.

Your part and hairline need the same attention. Dermatologist Dendy Engelman, MD, answered this for the [Skin Cancer Foundation](https://www.skincancer.org/blog/ask-the-expert-how-can-i-protect-my-scalp-better/). Her advice is to apply sunscreen "when hair is dry to wherever your hair parts, any thinning or balding spots and all around the hairline." Braids, cornrows and twists can mean several parts, so count every one. Engelman also says you can layer on cosmetic foundations and powders with SPF 15 and higher, as you would on your face.

A quick checklist before the first tee:

- Tops and backs of your ears
- The back of your neck
- Every part line
- Your hairline, front and back
- Your lips

## Which hat helps most?

For your scalp, a hat labeled UPF 50+ is "probably the best protection," in Engelman's words. For your face, ears and neck, the brim matters more than the fabric.

The Skin Cancer Foundation's [clothing guide](https://www.skincancer.org/prevention/sun-protection/clothing/clothing-umbrella-sun-protection/) recommends "a brim of at least three inches to shade the face, scalp, neck, shoulders and upper back." Its golf advice admits a wide-brimmed hat "isn't the most practical option on a golf course," and calls caps and visors good options that stay in place. The [CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/skin-cancer/sun-safety/index.html) adds that if you wear a baseball cap, you should cover your ears and the back of your neck with clothing, use sunscreen, or stay in the shade.

How each style compares:

- **A cap** covers your crown and part. Your ears and neck still need sunscreen.
- **A bucket hat** shades more of your face, ears and neck than a cap, [Druids](https://www.druids.com/blogs/golf/golf-cap-vs-visor) notes, though it can feel warmer.
- **A visor** leaves your scalp open, so the part and hairline need sunscreen every time.

## Tee times, shade and the cart

Book early or late if you can. The Skin Cancer Foundation suggests rounds "very early in the morning or late in the afternoon when the sun is less intense." It also notes that "many golf courses offer discounted rates for late tee-off times."

The CDC says "UV rays are strongest from 10 a.m. to 4 p.m. daylight saving time (9 a.m. to 3 p.m. standard time)." A late-morning or midday tee time puts much of your round inside that window.

Shade helps, but it isn't a substitute. The CDC advises using sunscreen and protective clothing "even when you're in the shade." For the cart, the Skin Cancer Foundation mentions cart covers and umbrellas designed for UV protection.

Don't forget your eyes. The foundation suggests sunglasses that "block 99-100 percent of UV radiation." The FDA says to look for "UV400" or "100% UV protection" on the label, and notes that a dark tint doesn't tell you the level of UV protection.

## When should you see a dermatologist?

See a board-certified dermatologist if you find anything that looks like skin cancer. The AAD says that in people with darker skin tones, skin cancer "may show up in areas that receive little, if any, sun." Its list includes the palms, soles, fingers and toes, nails, and inside the mouth or on a lip. It also notes that in darker skin tones, skin cancer is "often advanced by the time it's diagnosed."

That's why the AAD recommends regular skin self-exams. For darker skin tones, it says to look for:

- A bump, spot or mole that's growing, bleeding, itching or changing in any way
- A sore that doesn't heal, or heals and comes back

The AAD also says to check your nails and feet carefully, and that regular checks "can help catch skin cancer early, when it's highly treatable." If something looks off, its advice is to make an appointment with a board-certified dermatologist.

This is general guidance, not medical advice. A board-certified dermatologist can advise on your skin.
